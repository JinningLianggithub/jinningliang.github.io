Dwarf Galaxies and Dark Matter

Ultra-diffuse galaxies (UDGs) — extremely low surface-brightness dwarf galaxies — span a remarkably wide range of dark matter content, from apparently dark-matter-dominated systems to startling cases with little or no dark matter at all. They often host anomalously rich globular cluster (GC) populations relative to their stellar mass, making the GC system a powerful probe of the host halo. In this work, we develop a new method to constrain the dark matter distribution of UDGs using the spatial and mass statistics of their GC populations — from imaging data alone, without requiring spectroscopy.

Constraining the Dark-Matter Distribution of NGC5846-UDG1 with Globular-Cluster Mass Segregation

Liang, J., Jiang, F., Danieli, S., Benson, A., Hopkins, P. 2024, ApJ, 964, 53. [ADS] [arXiv] [Code: GCevo]

The Key Observable: Radial Mass Segregation

The galaxy NGC5846-UDG1 (UDG1 hereafter) hosts roughly 54 GC candidates within twice its stellar half-light radius of \(R_e \approx 2\) kpc, with \(\sim 13\%\) of its total stellar light locked in GCs — far exceeding typical galaxies of comparable stellar mass. Its GC population displays a striking radial mass segregation: more massive GCs are systematically found closer to the galaxy centre. This pattern is a hallmark of dynamical-friction-driven orbital decay. Because the dynamical friction force on a GC scales with its mass, more massive GCs spiral inward faster, naturally producing mass segregation over a Hubble time. The degree of segregation therefore encodes the strength of dynamical friction and, through it, the density profile and mass of the host dark matter halo.

The key question is: given the present-day GC mass, size, and spatial distributions, what dark matter halo — and what initial GC population — is required to reproduce them? To answer this, we develop a semi-analytical model GCevo that self-consistently evolves a population of GC progenitors forward in time under three coupled physical processes: dynamical friction, tidal evolution, and two-body relaxation.

Model

I. Dynamical Friction and Orbital Decay

The deceleration of a GC moving through the host potential is given by the Chandrasekhar formula. For a GC of mass \(m\) moving at velocity \(v\) through a medium of local density \(\rho\):

\[ \mathbf{a}_\mathrm{DF} = -\frac{4\pi G^2 m\, \rho(r)\, \ln\Lambda}{v^2} \left[\mathrm{erf}(X) - \frac{2X}{\sqrt{\pi}} e^{-X^2}\right] \hat{v}, \quad X \equiv \frac{v}{\sqrt{2}\,\sigma} \tag{2} \]

where \(\sigma\) is the local velocity dispersion of the background particles and \(\ln\Lambda\) is the Coulomb logarithm. The choice of \(\ln\Lambda\) is critical: the standard assumption \(\ln\Lambda = \ln(M/m)\) overestimates friction and fails to reproduce core stalling — the well-known tendency of GC orbits to halt rather than continue decaying once they reach a constant-density core. Following Petts et al. (2015), we instead adopt a locally calibrated prescription:

\[ \ln\Lambda = \ln\left(\frac{b_\mathrm{max}}{r_{1/2}}\right), \quad b_\mathrm{max} = \frac{r}{\gamma+1} \tag{3} \]

where \(r_{1/2}\) is the GC half-mass radius, \(r\) is the galactocentric radius, and \(\gamma = -\mathrm{d}\ln\rho/\mathrm{d}\ln r\) is the local logarithmic slope of the host density profile. In a cored profile, \(\gamma \to 0\) near the centre and \(b_\mathrm{max} \to r\), so \(\ln\Lambda \to \ln(r/r_{1/2})\) and friction weakens dramatically, reproducing core stalling. This is a significant improvement over prior work that treated the Coulomb logarithm as constant.

II. Tidal Evolution and the Structure of GCs

As a GC orbits, the tidal force from the host potential strips stars beyond the instantaneous tidal radius. The tidal radius is set by the condition that the gravitational pull of the GC balances the tidal force from the host:

\[ r_t = r \left(\frac{m}{[2 - \gamma]\,M_\mathrm{tot}( Stars beyond \(r_t\) are lost, reducing the GC mass. The rate of tidal mass loss per orbit is modelled as

\[ \frac{\Delta m_\mathrm{tid}}{m} = -\zeta\, \frac{T_\mathrm{orb}}{t_\mathrm{tid}}, \quad t_\mathrm{tid} = \left(\frac{r_t}{r_{1/2}}\right)^{3/2} t_\mathrm{dyn} \tag{10} \]

where \(\zeta \approx 0.4\) is a stripping efficiency factor calibrated against \(N\)-body simulations, \(T_\mathrm{orb}\) is the orbital period, and \(t_\mathrm{dyn} = \sqrt{r_{1/2}^3/Gm}\) is the GC dynamical time. The GC half-mass radius \(r_{1/2}\) is updated at each step to reflect mass loss, assuming virial equilibrium is maintained. For a tidally limited cluster modelled with an Elson–Fall–Freeman (EFF) profile,

\[ \rho_\mathrm{EFF}(r) = \rho_0 \left(1 + \frac{r^2}{a^2}\right)^{-\gamma_\mathrm{EFF}/2}, \tag{11} \]

the half-mass radius and scale radius \(a\) are related by

\[ r_{1/2} = a\left(2^{2/(\gamma_\mathrm{EFF}-2)} - 1\right)^{1/2} \tag{12} \]

As mass is stripped, \(r_{1/2}\) shrinks proportionally to \(m^{1/3}\) (the tidal boundary condition), which in turn reduces \(\ln\Lambda\) and weakens future dynamical friction — a critical feedback loop between mass loss and orbital evolution:

\[ r_{1/2} \propto r_t \propto m^{1/3} \tag{13} \]

This coupling is the main reason why including tidal evolution alongside dynamical friction is essential: without it, the Coulomb logarithm is overestimated for low-mass GCs, leading to artificially fast orbital decay and incorrect mass segregation predictions.

III. Two-Body Relaxation and Internal Mass Loss

Within a GC, gravitational scattering between stars gradually heats the cluster, evaporating stars beyond the escape velocity. The half-mass relaxation timescale is

\[ t_\mathrm{rh} = \frac{0.138\, N^{1/2}\, r_{1/2}^{3/2}}{G^{1/2}\, \bar{m}^{1/2}\, \ln(0.4N)} \tag{14} \]

where \(N\) is the number of stars and \(\bar{m}\) is the mean stellar mass. The relaxation-driven mass loss rate is

\[ \frac{\mathrm{d}m}{\mathrm{d}t}\bigg|_\mathrm{relax} = -\frac{\mu_\mathrm{ev}\, m}{t_\mathrm{rh}} \tag{16} \]

where \(\mu_\mathrm{ev} \approx 0.0142\) is the dimensionless evaporation rate, calibrated against direct \(N\)-body integrations. The two-body relaxation also causes the GC to expand. For an isolated cluster, the half-mass radius grows at a rate tied to the energy budget:

\[ \frac{\mathrm{d}r_{1/2}}{\mathrm{d}t}\bigg|_\mathrm{relax} = \frac{\xi_\mathrm{e}\, r_{1/2}}{t_\mathrm{rh}} \tag{17} \]

where \(\xi_\mathrm{e} \approx 0.0116\) (Hénon 1965). However, this expansion is limited by the tidal boundary: once \(r_{1/2}\) approaches \(r_t\), the cluster is said to be "tidally limited" and tidal stripping rather than relaxation controls the size. The combined effect of relaxation and tidal evolution determines whether a GC expands, shrinks, or dissolves over a Hubble time:

\[ \frac{\mathrm{d}r_{1/2}}{\mathrm{d}t} = \min\!\left(\frac{\mathrm{d}r_{1/2}}{\mathrm{d}t}\bigg|_\mathrm{relax},\; \frac{\mathrm{d}r_{1/2}}{\mathrm{d}t}\bigg|_\mathrm{tid}\right) \tag{18} \]

The combined mass loss from both channels is

\[ \frac{\mathrm{d}m}{\mathrm{d}t} = \frac{\mathrm{d}m}{\mathrm{d}t}\bigg|_\mathrm{relax} + \frac{\mathrm{d}m}{\mathrm{d}t}\bigg|_\mathrm{tid} \tag{19} \]

This mass-dependent, orbit-dependent evolution is what links the present-day GC size–mass distribution to the initial conditions and the host potential: massive GCs lose little mass and migrate inward via friction; low-mass GCs expand through relaxation, get tidally stripped, and dissolve; intermediate GCs undergo a complex interplay of both.

IV. Initial Conditions and Host Potential

The model evolves a population of GC progenitors initialised \(\sim 10\) Gyr ago. Three aspects of the initial conditions are treated as free parameters constrained by the MCMC:

Initial mass function. The GC progenitors are drawn from a power-law initial mass function (ICMF) motivated by observations of young massive star clusters:

\[ \frac{\mathrm{d}N}{\mathrm{d}m} \propto m^{-\alpha},\quad m \in [m_\mathrm{min},\, m_\mathrm{max}] \tag{20} \]

with \(\alpha\) as a free parameter. The lower mass cutoff \(m_\mathrm{min}\) is set to \(10^4\,M_\odot\) and \(m_\mathrm{max}\) is treated as a free parameter, allowing the MCMC to constrain both the slope and the upper end of the initial mass spectrum.

Initial structure–mass relation. Young star clusters follow an observed relation between size and mass. Each GC progenitor is assigned a half-mass radius according to the empirical young-cluster size–mass relation (Brown & Gnedin 2021):

\[ r_{1/2,0} = A \left(\frac{m_0}{10^4\,M_\odot}\right)^{\eta} \tag{21} \]

where \(A \approx 1\) pc and \(\eta \approx 0.24\). This sets the initial tidal filling factor and therefore determines how rapidly each GC is affected by tidal stripping and two-body relaxation.

Initial spatial distribution. The GC progenitors are assumed to be isotropically distributed following a Hernquist profile:

\[ \rho_\mathrm{GC}(r) = \frac{M_\mathrm{GC}}{2\pi}\frac{a_\mathrm{GC}}{r(r+a_\mathrm{GC})^3} \tag{22} \]

with scale radius \(a_\mathrm{GC}\) as a free parameter. This allows the initial GC distribution to be more or less extended than the smooth stellar body of UDG1, whose stellar surface brightness is described by a Sérsic profile fit to the HST observations. The projected half-mass radius of the initial GC distribution is \(r_\mathrm{proj,1/2} = 1.82\,a_\mathrm{GC}\).

Host potential. The composite potential is the sum of a Hernquist stellar component, fit to UDG1's observed Sérsic profile, and a dark matter halo. We test both a cuspy NFW profile and a cored Burkert profile:

\[ \rho_\mathrm{NFW}(r) = \frac{\rho_s}{(r/r_s)(1+r/r_s)^2} \tag{26} \] \[ \rho_\mathrm{Bk}(r) = \frac{\rho_0\,r_0^3}{(r+r_0)(r^2+r_0^2)} \tag{27} \]

Each halo is parameterised by its virial mass \(M_{200}\) and concentration \(c = r_{200}/r_s\) for NFW, or core radius \(r_0\) for Burkert. Together with \(\alpha\), \(m_\mathrm{max}\), and \(a_\mathrm{GC}\), these form the free parameter vector sampled by MCMC.

MCMC Forward-Modelling and Results

We combine the GCevo model with MCMC (using emcee) to perform forward-modelling: for each proposed parameter set, we evolve the initial GC population forward for 10 Gyr and compare the resulting distributions of GC mass, half-mass radius, and projected galactocentric radius with the observed HST data of UDG1. The likelihood penalises deviations in all three distributions simultaneously, with each distribution compared via a Kolmogorov–Smirnov statistic. The full analysis is run independently for NFW and Burkert host halos.

Assuming zero mass segregation at birth — the most conservative hypothesis — the MCMC yields a consistent conclusion regardless of halo shape: UDG1 is dark-matter poor, with a virial mass well below what is expected from the stellar-to-halo-mass relation at its stellar mass, and its halo concentration is lower than the cosmological average. UDG1 sits \(\sim 2-3\sigma\) below both the stellar-to-halo-mass relation and the GC-abundance-halo-mass relation. The latter is an important warning: naively applying the empirical relation between GC count and halo virial mass (calibrated over five dex for normal galaxies) to UDG1's rich GC system would imply a halo of \(\sim 10^{11}\,M_\odot\), whereas our dynamical model points to a significantly lower mass. The initial GC spatial distribution is found to be more extended than the smooth stellar body (\(a_\mathrm{GC}\) larger than the stellar Sérsic half-light radius), suggesting either an ex-situ origin via satellite disruption or an in-situ formation in an extended, high-velocity-dispersion starburst.
